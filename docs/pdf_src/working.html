This guide explains what the project does and how each part works. The detailed project report and the viva question bank are separate documents.

1. What this project is

Every five minutes, 207 loop detectors buried in the freeways of Los Angeles County report the average speed of the cars passing over them. This project asks one question: given the last hour of readings from every sensor, what will the speed be at every sensor over the next hour?

That is a spatio-temporal forecasting problem. It is temporal because each sensor's future depends on its own recent past and on the time of day and week. It is spatial because a traffic jam at one sensor spreads to its neighbours along the road. The project builds a ladder of six models, from a model that simply predicts "the usual speed for this time of week" up to Graph WaveNet, a graph neural network that learns how congestion moves through the road network. It then compares them fairly and tests whether the differences are statistically real.

Why it matters

Short-term speed forecasts drive route guidance (navigation apps), ramp metering, incident response and travel-time signs. A forecast 15 to 60 minutes ahead gives operators time to act before a jam forms.

What makes it hard

  • Sudden changes. Speed can collapse from 65 to 20 mph within minutes when congestion starts.
  • Strong daily and weekly cycles. Weekday evenings are slow, weekends are not.
  • Congestion spreads along roads. What happens downstream now often reaches a sensor later.
  • Missing data. 8.1% of readings are missing because sensors fail. They are stored as 0 mph.

4. How each part works

4.1 Loading and cleaning (src/traffic/data.py)

The speed file was written by a very old pandas version (0.15) that modern pandas cannot read, so load_speed() reads the raw arrays with h5py. Zeros are kept as zeros: they are a flag for "missing". Later steps either skip them (losses, metrics) or fill them with the last known value (features). They are never filled from the future.

4.2 Turning a time series into training examples

Each example is a window anchored at a time $t$. The input is the 12 readings from $t-55$ min to $t$; the target is the 12 readings from $t+5$ to $t+60$ min, for all 207 sensors at once.

Windows are split by time, never shuffled: the first 70% train the models (1 Mar – 23 May), the next 10% are for tuning (validation, to 4 Jun) and the last 20% are the untouched test set (4 – 27 Jun). That gives 23,974 / 3,425 / 6,850 windows, exactly the split used in the published papers, so results can be compared directly. Shuffling would leak: neighbouring windows share 11 of their 12 inputs.

4.3 The road graph

Sensors are nodes; the road distance $d_{ij}$ from sensor $i$ to sensor $j$ becomes an edge weight with a Gaussian kernel:

$$W_{ij} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{d_{ij}^2}{\sigma^2}\right)\ \text{ if } \ge 0.1,\ \text{ else } 0$$

Close sensors get weights near 1; far ones are cut to 0 to keep the graph sparse (1,515 directed edges, plus self-loops = 1,722 non-zeros, identical to the original DCRNN preprocessing). Road distance depends on direction, so the graph is directed: 73% of edges are one-way. Two "random-walk" matrices are derived from it: $P_f$ passes information downstream and $P_b$ passes it upstream.

4.4 Feature engineering (src/traffic/features.py)

The classical models need hand-made inputs. Each row is one (time, sensor) pair, and the label is the speed $h$ steps later. There are 34 features in six groups, and every feature uses only information available at time $t$ (a unit test proves this by overwriting the future and checking nothing changes).

4.6 Inside Graph WaveNet (src/traffic/models/gwnet.py)

  • Gated temporal convolution: $h = \tanh(\Theta_1 \star x)\odot\sigma(\Theta_2 \star x)$. The sigmoid "gate" decides how much signal passes, like an LSTM gate, but all time steps are computed in parallel. With dilations 1 and 2 in four blocks, every output sees the whole input hour (receptive field 13 ≥ 12).
  • Diffusion graph convolution: each sensor mixes in information from up to 2 hops downstream ($P_f$) and upstream ($P_b$), like traffic moving along the roads.
  • Self-adaptive adjacency: $\tilde A = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathrm{ReLU}(E_1E_2))$, a third graph learned from two small embedding matrices (207 × 10). It can capture links the road graph misses.

4.7 Training (src/traffic/trainer.py)

Inputs have two channels per sensor and step: speed, standardised with the training mean and standard deviation (58.58 ± 12.83 mph), and time of day in [0, 1). The model's outputs are converted back to mph, and the loss is the masked mean absolute error, which skips missing targets:

$$\mathcal{L}=\frac{\sum m_{t,i}\,|\hat y_{t,i}-y_{t,i}|}{\sum m_{t,i}},\qquad m_{t,i}=\mathbb 1[y_{t,i}>0]$$

Optimiser: Adam (learning rate 0.001, weight decay 0.0001), batch size 32, gradient clipping at 5. After every epoch the validation MAE is computed. The best weights are saved to checkpoints/, and training stops early if validation does not improve for 8 epochs. Training uses the Apple M3 GPU (MPS) when available, otherwise CUDA or the CPU.

4.8 Evaluation (src/traffic/metrics.py, scripts/evaluate.py)

  • MAE (average absolute error, mph), RMSE (penalises big misses) and MAPE (error in %), computed only where a real reading exists.
  • Reported at 15, 30 and 60 minutes and averaged over all 12 steps, the same protocol as the literature.
  • Diebold–Mariano test: is one model's error significantly lower than another's? A Newey–West correction accounts for overlapping windows.
  • Block bootstrap: 95% confidence intervals made by resampling whole test days.

The main findings, in one paragraph each
  • Graph WaveNet is best, and significantly so. Its average error is 3.07 mph, 4.5% below gradient boosting. Diebold–Mariano tests reject equal accuracy at all three horizons (p < 0.001), and the result is within 0.03–0.06 mph of the published Graph WaveNet numbers despite a shorter training run.
  • Good features get a classical model most of the way. Gradient boosting with the 34 engineered features (3.22 mph) beats the published deep FC-LSTM at every horizon and is close behind Graph WaveNet at 60 minutes (3.66 vs 3.59 mph). Our Graph WaveNet also beats the published DCRNN at all three horizons.
  • "The usual speed for this time of week" is the single most useful feature (permutation importance), especially 60 minutes ahead, where the current state matters less.
  • Missing data hurts the deep model more. With the latest reading missing, Graph WaveNet's 60-minute error is 5.36 mph against gradient boosting's 4.50.

6. The interactive web app

app/app.py turns the trained Graph WaveNet into a page you can explore at http://localhost:8501. It is built with Streamlit (page and controls), Altair (interactive charts) and pydeck (the map).

How it works

  1. On start-up it loads the dataset and the Graph WaveNet checkpoint once and keeps them in memory (st.cache_resource), picking the GPU when one is available.
  2. When you click Run forecast it finds the test window for the chosen date and time, runs the model on that one window (about 6 ms on the M3 GPU), and draws the result. Nothing recalculates until you click, so you can change several settings first.
  3. Quick-action buttons apply immediately: step ±5 minutes or ±1 hour, jump to the morning rush, midday, evening rush or night, or let Play through the day advance the clock every second.
  4. Find a sensor buttons pick the slowest sensor now, the one forecast to slow down most, the one with the worst forecast this hour, or a random one. You can also click any dot on the map.
  5. Whole day shows the forecast made 15 and 60 minutes earlier against what happened, for every 5-minute step of the day.
  6. Model accuracy shows the saved test-set scores of Graph WaveNet and the baselines (from results/metrics/), by MAE, RMSE or MAPE.

Glossary

Loop detectorA wire loop in the road surface that senses passing cars; here it reports 5-minute average speed.
HorizonHow far ahead the forecast is (5 to 60 minutes; step 1 to 12).
AnchorThe "now" time of a window: the last input step.
MaskingLeaving missing (0) values out of the loss and the scores.
LeakageAccidentally letting information from the future (or the test set) into training. It makes scores look better than they really are.
AblationRemoving or adding one component at a time to measure what it contributes.
CheckpointThe saved model weights from the epoch with the best validation score.
MAE / RMSE / MAPEMean absolute error (mph), root mean squared error (mph), mean absolute percentage error (%).
